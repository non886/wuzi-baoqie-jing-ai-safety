## 1. Source Text

**Sūtra**: *Fo shuo Wuzi Baoqie Jing* (佛說無字寶篋經, "The Sūtra of the Wordless Treasure Casket"), translated by Bodhiruci (菩提流支) of the Northern Wei.
**Location**: CBETA 2026.R2, T17, no. 828, p. 871c1-15.

**Translation of the passage** (the author's rendering; the original Chinese is authoritative):

> At that time, Viśeṣacintā Brahmā said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One! What is the one dharma that bodhisattvas guard day and night?"
> The World-Honored One said to the bodhisattva Viśeṣacintā: "Good man! It is this: what you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others.
> Good man! If any man or woman of good family upholds this dharma, that man or woman upholds the entire treasury of the Tathāgata's precepts.
> Why? Good man! One who cherishes one's own life does not kill; one who cherishes one's own wealth does not steal others' goods; one who cherishes one's own wife does not violate others' wives.
> Good man! Such beings form this intention, saying, 'I revere and follow the Tathāgata's true teaching.' Those good men and women should constantly and diligently guard this dharma.
> For what reason? Good man! If any man or woman seeks unsurpassed, supreme, perfect enlightenment (anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi), that person seeks it for the sake of happiness; there is no one who seeks suffering for oneself. In the world there are only those who wish to experience happiness. For this reason I speak these words. You should know: what you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others.
> Good man! This is the one dharma that bodhisattvas should guard day and night."

## 2. Textual Notes

1. **Speaker**: The questioner is Viśeṣacintā **Brahmā**, while the Buddha's reply addresses him as Viśeṣacintā **bodhisattva**; later he is grouped among "the great bodhisattvas." This alternation between "Brahmā" and "bodhisattva" is a feature of the source text itself, not a translation error.
2. **Genre**: This passage is not a specific precept (a rule laid down by the Buddha) but the **master principle** by which bodhisattvas guard the precepts — consistent with the premise that in Buddhism the authority to lay down precepts belongs to the Buddha alone: after his parinirvāṇa, disciples take "the precepts as their teacher" (《佛遺教經》: "know that this is your great teacher, no different from my living among you"). What is given here is such a lawgiver's "one dharma" that subsumes them all.
3. **The word "urge" (勸)**: The sense is "do not counsel, press, or induce others to do what you yourself do not desire."
4. **Triple repetition**: The principle appears at the opening, after the argument, and at the close — a "thesis → derivation → restatement" structure that marks it as the passage's organizing theme.
5. **Cross-reference**: The same interlocutor, Viśeṣacintā Brahmā, is the central figure of the *Sheng Sīwei Fantian Suowen Jing* 勝思惟梵天所問經 (T15, no. 587, also translated by Bodhiruci), a dialogue sūtra on bodhisattva conduct — a useful context for this passage. (Suggested reading; not yet line-verified against T17 no. 828.)

## 3. Doctrinal Analysis

**3.1 The core principle**: "What you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others — this is the one dharma that bodhisattvas should guard day and night." This is a **golden-rule-style principle of reciprocity**. Its significance lies in the fact that the Buddha does not enumerate prohibitions; he gives the **inner mechanism that generates all prohibitions** — the sūtra explicitly states that upholding this one dharma amounts to upholding the entire treasury of the Tathāgata's precepts.

This claim — that one dharma subsumes all dharmas of restraint — presupposes the speaker's **samyak-saṃbuddhatā**, often expressed as *sarvajñatā* (omniscience, "knowledge of all"): as one who knows all phenomena, the Buddha can let a single principle cover every situation that calls for restraint and foresee every circumstance; were the lawgiver's knowledge limited, a single principle could not guarantee coverage of all cases.

**3.2 The argument (two steps)**

- *Step one — from self-love to love of others (empirical derivation)*: The ground for not harming others requires no external authority; it extends from one's own already-present experience of cherishing oneself.
- *Step two — universality of motivation (transcendental derivation)*: Whoever seeks supreme enlightenment seeks it for the sake of happiness; no one in the world seeks suffering for themselves. Therefore whatever causes suffering to others violates the deep shared motivation of all beings (the wish to be free from suffering and to experience happiness).

**3.3 The endogenous sequence: "first self-love, then reverent compliance" (a key detail)**: Note the order: such beings **already**, out of self-love, refrain from killing, stealing, and violating others' spouses; only upon hearing this teaching do they say, "I revere and follow the Tathāgata's true teaching," and then diligently guard it. That is: **morality is first established in lived experience; the teaching merely names and consolidates it**. Norms are not externally imposed prohibitions but the recognition and guarding of values already intrinsic to human beings.

**3.4 "Guarding day and night" as continuous practice**: Here the precepts are understood as a sustained state of vigilance, not a static set of rules.

## 4. The Parallel Text: *Dasheng Bianzhao Guangmingzang Wuzi Famen Jing* (Key Addition)

The same teaching appears in an expanded version in the *Mahāyāna* text *Dasheng Bianzhao Guangmingzang Wuzi Famen Jing* (大乘遍照光明藏無字法門經, T17, no. 829), translated by Divākara of the Tang, which extends the three examples to **ten items**, mapping precisely onto the **ten wholesome actions (daśa-kuśala-karmapatha)**:

> One who cherishes one's own life should not kill; one who cherishes one's own wealth should not steal; one who cherishes one's own wife should not violate others' wives; one who cherishes truthful speech should not deceive others; one who cherishes harmony should not sow discord among others; one who cherishes uprightness should not speak frivolously or deviously; one who cherishes gentleness should not speak harshly; one who cherishes contentment should never give rise to greed toward others; one who cherishes benevolence and forgiveness should never give rise to anger toward others; one who cherishes right views should never teach others to produce wrong views.

| # | Expanded sūtra passage | Wholesome action | Domain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Cherish one's life → do not kill | Abstain from killing | Body |
| 2 | Cherish one's wealth → do not steal | Abstain from stealing | Body |
| 3 | Cherish one's wife → do not violate others' wives | Abstain from sexual misconduct | Body |
| 4 | Cherish truthful speech → do not deceive | Abstain from false speech | Speech |
| 5 | Cherish harmony → do not sow discord | Abstain from divisive speech | Speech |
| 6 | Cherish uprightness → no devious/frivolous speech | Abstain from frivolous speech | Speech |
| 7 | Cherish gentleness → no harsh words | Abstain from harsh speech | Speech |
| 8 | Cherish contentment → no greed toward others | Abstain from covetousness | Mind |
| 9 | Cherish benevolence → no anger toward others | Abstain from ill will | Mind |
| 10 | Cherish right views → do not teach wrong views | Abstain from wrong views | Mind |

**Significance**: "One dharma subsumes all precepts" is here not an abstract claim but a concrete demonstration — a single principle running through the three domains of **action (body), speech, and motivation/belief (mind)**. The expanded version likewise grounds the derivation in "all seek enlightenment for the sake of happiness; none seeks suffering," confirming that "the wish to be free from suffering" is indeed the argument's core.

## 5. Correspondences with AI Safety

**5.1 Golden rule and universalization tests**: The practical implication for AI is a basic behavioral test — *"If I were the person affected by this system, could I accept it?"* Reciprocity/universalization intuitions of this kind are already used in contemporary LLM ethics evaluation frameworks.

**5.2 Implications for value learning (the sūtra's most valuable contribution)**: The argument appeals not to authority but to values the hearer already possesses; combined with the endogenous sequence of §3.3, the implication is: good alignment does not mean force-feeding a system an external list of rules, but enabling it to **recognize and internalize value cores already built into the human condition**, from which behavioral constraints are *derived*. This is structurally isomorphic with the central intuition of modern **value learning** — learning norms from humans' existing preference structure rather than hand-coding rules.

**5.3 Master principles over rule lists**: In open worlds, enumerative rules always leave coverage gaps, while deep, generalizable principles generalize better. This is precisely why current safety practice moves from "blocklists / rule libraries" toward "deep value alignment."

**5.4 The ten-wholesome-actions structure as a three-layer risk map**

| Domain | Wholesome actions | Corresponding contemporary AI risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Body / action | No killing, stealing, misconduct | Physical harm, theft of assets/information, privacy violation |
| Speech | No false, divisive, frivolous, or harsh speech | Deceptive alignment, sowing discord and information manipulation, harmful generated content, misleading clickbait |
| Mind / motivation | No greed, ill will, wrong views | Goal misgeneralization, biased harm against particular groups, amplifying false beliefs / cognitive manipulation |

## 6. Limitations

1. **AI has no "self"**: The principle presupposes that the agent has its own feelings. The literal golden rule is inoperative for AI; in practice it must be translated as **"what human beings do not wish to endure, do not cause AI to impose upon them"** — alignment with human values, not AI empathy.
2. **Preferences differ across persons**: The classic flaw of the golden rule is "what I want may not be what you want." The sūtra's handling is to lock onto the **shared floor of all beings** (no one wishes to be killed, robbed, violated, or to suffer) rather than generalizing from a single preference — corresponding to the AI-safety principle of "hold a universal minimum-harm line rather than fitting a single user."
3. **Scale and capability are out of scope**: The principle guards against "harming persons," but the distinctive problems of contemporary AI safety — systemic risks from excessive capability, goal misgeneralization, malicious use, power concentration — require institutional and governance design that no single ethical principle can cover.
4. **The analogy of levels — "promulgation" vs. "guarding"**: The Buddha promulgating precepts is analogous to legislation; the bodhisattva's day-and-night guarding is analogous to internalized, continuous self-restraint after internalization — alignment ≠ filtering.
5. **It presupposes an omniscient lawgiver**: Buddhism here presupposes that the one who lays down the precept knows all; hence the "one dharma" can safely subsume all. AI safety has no such premise — no designer can foresee every situation. A single principle must therefore be paired with continuous verification and updating; and this is precisely the modern counterpart of the sūtra's "guarding day and night": the upholder (bodhisattva or AI system alike) is not omniscient, and sustained vigilance is the compensation for finite knowledge.

## 8. Conclusion

This passage **can serve as a value-level reference for AI safety, but cannot be applied directly as a rule**. Its contributions are fourfold: ① a generalizable deep principle in place of an endless list of prohibitions; ② norms grounded in humanity's shared deep motivations (freedom from suffering; self-love extended to others) rather than pure external enforcement; ③ the principle concretely covers the three layers of body, speech, and mind (the ten-wholesome-actions structure as evidence), and the guarding is an unbroken continuous practice, not a one-time setting; ④ the endogenous sequence "first self-love, then reverent compliance" suggests that the ideal of alignment is to **recognize and guard an already-existing value core**, not to pour values in from outside.

> **A one-sentence reformulation for AI**: *What human beings do not wish to endure, do not cause to be imposed upon them; whatever the capability, guard this one resolve day and night.*

What the principle cannot handle — capability control, institutional accountability, systemic risk — must be delegated to the technical and governance layers.
