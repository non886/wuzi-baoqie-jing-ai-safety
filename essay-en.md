## 1. Source Text and Textual Background

### 1.1 Source Text Passage (CBETA T17 no. 828 & Tibetan Toh 259 Parallel)

**Sūtra**: *Fo shuo Wuzi Baoqie Jing* (佛說無字寶篋經, "The Sūtra of the Wordless Treasure Casket"), translated by Bodhiruci (菩提流支) of the Northern Wei[cite: 2].
**Location**: CBETA 2026.R2, T17, no. 828, p. 871c1-15[cite: 2].

**Translation of the Chinese passage (Author's rendering from T828)**[cite: 2]:

> At that time, Viśeṣacintā Brahmā said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One! What is the one dharma that bodhisattvas guard day and night?"[cite: 2]
> The World-Honored One said to the bodhisattva Viśeṣacintā: "Good man! It is this: what you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others[cite: 2].
> Good man! If any man or woman of good family upholds this dharma, that man or woman upholds the entire treasury of the Tathāgata's precepts[cite: 2].
> Why? Good man! One who cherishes one's own life does not kill; one who cherishes one's own wealth does not steal others' goods; one who cherishes one's own wife does not violate others' wives[cite: 2].
> Good man! Such beings form this intention, saying, 'I revere and follow the Tathāgata's true teaching.' Those good men and women should constantly and diligently guard this dharma[cite: 2].
> For what reason? Good man! If any man or woman seeks unsurpassed, supreme, perfect enlightenment (anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi), that person seeks it for the sake of happiness; there is no one who seeks suffering for oneself. In the world there are only those who wish to experience happiness. For this reason I speak these words. You should know: what you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others[cite: 2].
> Good man! This is the one dharma that bodhisattvas should guard day and night."[cite: 2]

*(Comparative Reference: 84000 translation from Tibetan Kangyur Toh 259)*:
> *"Viśiṣṭacinti, whatever you yourself do not desire, do not apply it to others. Why? If you ask who guards the entire collection of the Tathāgata’s training..."*

### 1.2 Sanskrit Title, Tibetan Version, and Fragmentary Manuscript Evidence

According to the DILA catalog and research published by 84000 (Toh 259), Chinese Taishō nos. 828, 829, and 830 represent parallel or closely related translations of the same underlying text.

* **Sanskrit Title**: *Āryānakṣarakaraṇḍakavairocanagarbha-nāma-mahāyānasūtra* (short form: *Anakṣarakaraṇḍakavairocanagarbha*).
* **Tibetan Version**: Preserved in the Kangyur under **Toh 259**:
  `འཕགས་པ་ཡི་གེ་མེད་པའི་ཟ་མ་ཏོག་རྣམ་པར་སྣང་མཛད་ཀྱི་སྙིང་པོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ཐེག་པ་ཆེན་པོའི་མདོ།`
* **Status of Sanskrit Manuscripts**:
  There is currently **no known complete Sanskrit manuscript** for this sūtra. However, both its Sanskrit title (*Āryānakṣarakaraṇḍakavairocanagarbha-nāma-mahāyānasūtra*) and a **fragmentary manuscript leaf** are well documented. Scholars identified a single folio of Sanskrit fragments embedded within the Stein collection at the British Library (**Or.15010**, originally cataloged among *Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* fragments), corresponding precisely to the concluding portion of the text (Toh 259, folios 263b.2–264a.3). Thus, the precise philological statement is: **The sūtra possesses an established Sanskrit title and surviving Sanskrit manuscript fragments, though no complete Sanskrit manuscript has been recovered.**

## 2. Textual Notes

1. **Speaker**: The questioner is Viśeṣacintā **Brahmā**, while the Buddha's reply addresses him as Viśeṣacintā **bodhisattva**; later he is grouped among "the great bodhisattvas."[cite: 2] This alternation between "Brahmā" and "bodhisattva" is a feature of the source text itself, not a translation error[cite: 2].
2. **Genre**: This passage is not a specific precept (a rule laid down by the Buddha) but the **master principle** by which bodhisattvas guard the precepts — consistent with the premise that in Buddhism the authority to lay down precepts belongs to the Buddha alone: after his parinirvāṇa, disciples take "the precepts as their teacher" (《佛遺教經》: "know that this is your great teacher, no different from my living among you")[cite: 2]. What is given here is such a lawgiver's "one dharma" that subsumes them all[cite: 2].
3. **The word "urge" (勸)**: The sense is "do not counsel, press, or induce others to do what you yourself do not desire."[cite: 2]
4. **Triple repetition**: The principle appears at the opening, after the argument, and at the close — a "thesis → derivation → restatement" structure that marks it as the passage's organizing theme[cite: 2].
5. **Cross-reference**: The same interlocutor, Viśeṣacintā Brahmā, is the central figure of the *Sheng Sīwei Fantian Suowen Jing* 勝思惟梵天所問經 (T15, no. 587, also translated by Bodhiruci), a dialogue sūtra on bodhisattva conduct[cite: 2].

## 3. Doctrinal Analysis

**3.1 The core principle**: "What you yourself do not desire, do not urge upon others — this is the one dharma that bodhisattvas should guard day and night."[cite: 2] This is a **golden-rule-style principle of reciprocity**[cite: 2]. Its significance lies in the fact that the Buddha does not enumerate prohibitions; he gives the **inner mechanism that generates all prohibitions** — the sūtra explicitly states that upholding this one dharma amounts to upholding the entire treasury of the Tathāgata's precepts[cite: 2].

This claim — that one dharma subsumes all dharmas of restraint — presupposes the speaker's state of supreme enlightenment (*samyak-saṃbodhi*) and omniscience (*sarvajñatā*): as one who knows all phenomena, the Buddha can let a single principle cover every situation that calls for restraint; were the lawgiver's knowledge limited, a single principle could not guarantee coverage of all cases[cite: 2].

**3.2 The argument (two steps)**[cite: 2]
- *Step one — from self-love to love of others (empirical derivation)*: The ground for not harming others requires no external authority; it extends from one's own already-present experience of cherishing oneself[cite: 2].
- *Step two — universality of motivation (transcendental derivation)*: Whoever seeks supreme enlightenment seeks it for the sake of happiness; no one in the world seeks suffering for themselves[cite: 2]. Therefore whatever causes suffering to others violates the deep shared motivation of all beings (the wish to be free from suffering and to experience happiness)[cite: 2].

**3.3 The endogenous sequence: "first self-love, then reverent compliance"**: Note the order: such beings **already**, out of self-love, refrain from killing, stealing, and violating others' spouses; only upon hearing this teaching do they say, "I revere and follow the Tathāgata's true teaching," and then diligently guard it[cite: 2]. That is: **morality is first established in lived experience; the teaching merely names and consolidates it**[cite: 2]. Norms are not externally imposed prohibitions but the recognition and guarding of intrinsic values[cite: 2].

**3.4 "Guarding day and night" as continuous practice**: Here the precepts are understood as a sustained state of vigilance, not a static set of rules[cite: 2].

## 4. The Parallel Text: *Dasheng Bianzhao Guangmingzang Wuzi Famen Jing* (Key Addition)

The same teaching appears in an expanded version in the *Mahāyāna* text *Dasheng Bianzhao Guangmingzang Wuzi Famen Jing* (大乘遍照光明藏無字法門經, T17, no. 829), translated by Divākara of the Tang, which extends the three examples to **ten items**, mapping precisely onto the **ten wholesome actions (daśa-kuśala-karmapatha)**[cite: 2]:

| # | Expanded sūtra passage | Wholesome action | Domain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Cherish one's life → do not kill | Abstain from killing | Physical (Body) |
| 2 | Cherish one's wealth → do not steal | Abstain from stealing | Physical (Body) |
| 3 | Cherish one's wife → do not violate others' wives | Abstain from sexual misconduct | Physical (Body) |
| 4 | Cherish truthful speech → do not deceive | Abstain from false speech | Verbal (Speech) |
| 5 | Cherish harmony → do not sow discord | Abstain from divisive speech | Verbal (Speech) |
| 6 | Cherish uprightness → no devious/frivolous speech | Abstain from frivolous speech | Verbal (Speech) |
| 7 | Cherish gentleness → no harsh words | Abstain from harsh speech | Verbal (Speech) |
| 8 | Cherish contentment → no greed toward others | Abstain from covetousness | Mental (Mind) |
| 9 | Cherish benevolence → no anger toward others | Abstain from ill will | Mental (Mind) |
| 10 | Cherish right views → do not teach wrong views | Abstain from wrong views | Mental (Mind) |

**Significance**: "One dharma subsumes all precepts" is a concrete demonstration — a single principle running through the three domains of **action (body), speech, and motivation/belief (mind)**[cite: 2].

## 5. Correspondences with AI Safety

**5.1 Golden rule and universalization tests**: The practical implication for AI is a basic behavioral test — *"If I were the person affected by this system, could I accept it?"*[cite: 2]

**5.2 Implications for value learning**: Good alignment does not mean force-feeding a system an external list of rules, but enabling it to **recognize and internalize value cores already built into the human condition**, from which behavioral constraints are *derived*[cite: 2]. This is structurally isomorphic with modern **value learning**[cite: 2].

**5.3 Master principles over rule lists**: In open worlds, enumerative rules always leave coverage gaps, while deep, generalizable principles generalize better[cite: 2].

**5.4 The ten-wholesome-actions structure as a three-layer risk map**[cite: 2]

| Domain | Wholesome actions | Corresponding contemporary AI risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical (Body) | No killing, stealing, misconduct | Physical harm, theft of assets/information, privacy violation |
| Verbal (Speech) | No false, divisive, frivolous, or harsh speech | Deceptive alignment, sowing discord and information manipulation, harmful generated content |
| Mental (Mind) | No greed, ill will, wrong views | Goal misgeneralization, biased harm against particular groups, amplifying false beliefs / cognitive manipulation |

## 6. Limitations

1. **AI has no "self"**: Translates in practice as **"what human beings do not wish to endure, do not cause AI to impose upon them."**[cite: 2]
2. **Preferences differ across persons**: Locked onto the **shared floor of all beings** (universal minimum harm) rather than individual preferences[cite: 2].
3. **Scale and capability are out of scope**: Systemic capability risks, power concentration, and governance require engineering and legal frameworks beyond single ethical principles[cite: 2].
4. **The analogy of levels**: Legislation/alignment training vs. continuous internal vigilance[cite: 2].
5. **Presupposition of an omniscient lawgiver**: Unlike human system designers, the Buddha is presupposed as omniscient[cite: 2]. AI alignment must compensate for non-omniscience through continuous verification, which mirrors the sūtra's call to "guard day and night."[cite: 2]
